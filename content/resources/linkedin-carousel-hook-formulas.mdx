# LinkedIn Carousel Hook Formulas That Stop the Scroll

The first slide of a LinkedIn carousel is the only slide most people see. If the hook does not stop the scroll, the rest of the carousel does not matter.

This guide gives you 12 LinkedIn carousel hook formulas you can reuse. Each formula includes why it works, when to use it, and a real example you can adapt for your own content.

## Key Takeaways

- The hook slide should promise a specific outcome, not a generic topic.
- The best hooks speak to a pain, a curiosity gap, or a recent change.
- Formulas make hook writing faster without making your content sound identical.
- BrandOps applies these hooks automatically when generating carousels from research.
- Testing two hooks on similar topics is the fastest way to improve swipe rate.

## What a LinkedIn Carousel Hook Actually Does

A hook has one job: make the reader swipe to slide 2. It does not need to explain the whole idea. It needs to create enough curiosity or urgency that swiping feels like less effort than scrolling past.

Strong hooks do one of these:

- Name a specific pain the reader feels
- Point out a change the reader cannot ignore
- Promise a concrete result in a short time
- Challenge a belief the reader holds
- Reveal a gap between what most people do and what works

## 12 LinkedIn Carousel Hook Formulas

### 1. The Mistake Formula

**Structure:** "Most people [do X]. Here is why it fails, and what to do instead."

**Why it works:** readers want to know if they are making the mistake.

**Example:** "Most LinkedIn carousels start with the problem. That is why readers scroll past. Start with the outcome instead."

**Best for:** educational content, contrarian takes, common misconceptions.

### 2. The Number Formula

**Structure:** "[N] [things/ways/mistakes] that [outcome]."

**Why it works:** numbers set clear expectations and promise completeness.

**Example:** "5 carousel hook formulas that doubled my swipe rate."

**Best for:** listicles, frameworks, step-by-step breakdowns.

### 3. The Change Formula

**Structure:** "[Recent change] just changed [topic]. Here is what to do about it."

**Why it works:** timely content feels more valuable than evergreen advice.

**Example:** "LinkedIn just changed how carousels appear in the feed. Your first slide now matters more than ever."

**Best for:** news-driven content, platform updates, trend responses.

### 4. The Contrarian Formula

**Structure:** "[Common advice] is wrong. Here is what actually works."

**Why it works:** disagreement creates curiosity and emotional engagement.

**Example:** "You do not need more design tools. You need fewer words on slide 1."

**Best for:** hot takes, debunking myths, strong opinions.

### 5. The Promise Formula

**Structure:** "How to [specific result] in [time frame] without [common objection]."

**Why it works:** specific promises feel actionable and achievable.

**Example:** "How to write a week of LinkedIn hooks in 30 minutes without sounding generic."

**Best for:** tutorials, productivity content, workflow guides.

### 6. The Question Formula

**Structure:** "[Question that names a pain or gap]?"

**Why it works:** questions make the reader answer mentally, which creates engagement.

**Example:** "Why do some carousels get thousands of swipes while yours gets ignored?"

**Best for:** diagnostic content, problem-solution posts.

### 7. The Comparison Formula

**Structure:** "[Thing A] vs. [Thing B]: which one actually [outcome]?"

**Why it works:** readers love verdicts. They want to know the winner.

**Example:** "AI-generated hooks vs. human-written hooks: which one stops the scroll?"

**Best for:** comparison content, tool reviews, method debates.

### 8. The Personal Story Formula

**Structure:** "I [did something counterintuitive]. Here is what happened."

**Why it works:** personal stories are more memorable than abstract advice.

**Example:** "I stopped writing hooks and started copying formulas. My carousel engagement tripled."

**Best for:** founder stories, lessons learned, case studies.

### 9. The One-Liner Formula

**Structure:** "[Short, punchy statement that reframes a topic]."

**Why it works:** brevity stands out in a crowded feed.

**Example:** "Your carousel is not a blog post. It is a billboard."

**Best for:** thought leadership, strong positioning.

### 10. The Curiosity Gap Formula

**Structure:** "[Surprising fact]. Here is the part nobody talks about."

**Why it works:** the reader wants to close the gap between what they know and what is hidden.

**Example:** "The highest-performing LinkedIn carousels have only 12 words on slide 1. Here is why."

**Best for:** data-driven content, behind-the-scenes insights.

### 11. The "I Wish I Knew" Formula

**Structure:** "I wish I knew [lesson] before [situation]."

**Why it works:** it combines personal experience with reader empathy.

**Example:** "I wish I knew these 3 hook rules before I posted my first 50 carousels."

**Best for:** lessons learned, beginner-friendly content.

### 12. The Outcome Formula

**Structure:** "[Specific audience] — here is how to [outcome] in [time frame]."

**Why it works:** it calls out the exact reader and promises a result.

**Example:** "Solo consultants — here is how to create 5 carousel hooks in 10 minutes."

**Best for:** niche audiences, offer-specific content.

## How to Choose the Right Hook Formula

Match the formula to your topic, not your mood:

| Topic type | Best formula | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common mistake | Mistake | Creates self-check |
| List or framework | Number | Sets expectations |
| News or update | Change | Feels urgent |
| Debated topic | Contrarian | Drives engagement |
| Tutorial | Promise | Feels actionable |
| Problem diagnosis | Question | Pulls reader in |
| Tool/method comparison | Comparison | Promises a verdict |
| Personal lesson | Personal story | Builds trust |
| Strong belief | One-liner | Stands out visually |
| Surprising insight | Curiosity gap | Makes reader curious |
| Beginner lesson | I wish I knew | Empathy-driven |
| Niche audience | Outcome | Direct relevance |

## Hook Writing Rules

### Keep it under 15 words when possible

The hook slide is not the place for nuance. One strong line beats three careful sentences.

### Lead with the reader, not yourself

Bad: "I want to share a carousel framework."
Good: "Your carousel hook is losing readers before slide 2. Here is how to fix it."

### Use specific numbers and time frames

"5 formulas" beats "several formulas."
"In 10 minutes" beats "quickly."

### Avoid AI-generic phrases

Cut: "In today's fast-paced world," "it is important to note," "unlock the power of."

### Make the swipe feel obvious

The reader should think: "I need to see slide 2."

## Common Hook Mistakes

### Starting with context

"LinkedIn carousels are a powerful content format..." — the reader already knows this. Start with the value.

### Being clever instead of clear

Puns and wordplay do not work if the reader does not understand the promise.

### Promising too much

"How to 10x your LinkedIn in 7 days" feels like clickbait unless you can prove it.

### Forgetting the swipe

A hook that answers the whole question on slide 1 removes the reason to swipe.

## FAQ

### What makes a LinkedIn carousel hook effective?

A good hook stops the scroll by promising specific value, naming a pain, or creating a curiosity gap in under 15 words.

### How long should a hook slide be?

Aim for 8 to 15 words on the hook slide. It should be readable in one glance on mobile.

### Should I use the same hook formula every time?

No. Rotate formulas based on topic type. Repetition makes your content predictable.

### Can AI write good LinkedIn hooks?

Yes, if it has a formula library and understands the research topic. Human review is still essential to match the hook to your voice.

### What is the best hook formula for beginners?

The **Mistake** formula is the safest starting point: "Most people [do X]. Here is why it fails."

## Key Takeaway

A LinkedIn carousel lives or dies on slide 1. Use a proven hook formula, keep it specific and short, and always make the reader want to see slide 2. Test different formulas on similar topics to learn what stops the scroll for your audience.
